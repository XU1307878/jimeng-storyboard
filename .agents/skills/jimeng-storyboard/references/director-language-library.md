# Director language decision library

Use this reference to choose camera, focus, composition, lighting and detail treatment from the script's dramatic function. It is a decision library, not a menu that must be exhausted.

## 1. Dramatic shot progression

A scene may progress through four useful functions:

1. **Context**: establish environment, geography, atmosphere or threat.
2. **Action**: show the character entering, confronting, working or changing the situation.
3. **Decision**: isolate the reaction, emotional turn or relationship shift.
4. **Evidence**: end on the prop, wound, gesture, gaze or physical result that proves the change.

Choose only the functions that add information. A fast hook can begin on evidence or action and reveal context afterward. Do not mechanically write `远景→全景→中景→近景→特写`; vary scale only when the viewer's question changes.

## 2. Focus as narrative syntax

Focus changes must reveal or transfer attention:

- **Foreground focus**: establish a prop, obstruction, symbol or imminent danger before the character.
- **Subject focus**: place agency, performance and dialogue on the character.
- **Background focus**: reveal a watcher, destination, threat or power structure behind the foreground subject.
- **Rack focus**: use only when attention must visibly move between two depth planes. State the starting plane, ending plane and story reason.

Do not use rack focus as decoration. Preserve subject positions, lens direction and key-light continuity during the focus transfer.

## 3. Composition by relationship

- **Opposition**: two sides face across a stable axis; use balanced negative space or a confrontational two-shot.
- **Hierarchy**: use vertical placement, scale, foreground dominance or restrained low/high angle to show power.
- **Isolation**: place one subject off-center with environmental weight or empty space pressing toward them.
- **Group control**: arrange characters in a readable line, V, triangle, ring, staggered depth or opposing clusters. Assign one narrative center and keep supporting reactions lower in amplitude.
- **Dual world or mirrored state**: use symmetry only when the script contains a real parallel, split identity or opposing world. Do not add a portal or mirror merely for style.

For vertical 9:16, design depth vertically: foreground low, subject center, authority/threat high or deep. Avoid wide horizontal blocking that makes faces too small.

## 4. Lighting as physical storytelling

For every shot identify:

- motivated source: window, practical lamp, moon, fire, lava, screen, doorway, spotlight or reflected surface;
- direction and shadow destination;
- stable white balance and exposure;
- narrative purpose: safety, isolation, authority, revelation, danger or transition.

Useful treatments include side light for tension and texture, back/rim light for separation or mystery, volumetric beams when the environment physically supports dust/fog, cool light for distance or unease, and warm light for intimacy or relief. Lens flare, neon, fluorescence and laser-like beams are opt-in story elements, not generic quality terms.

Any light change needs a visible cause and timing. During internal cuts, preserve key-light direction and color unless the script contains a lighting event.

## 5. Action and spectacle

Name a stylized action camera only after defining subject path, impact and end state:

- chase follow: fixed following distance, readable screen direction, inertia on turns;
- pass-through combat: camera path passes a motivated gap without clipping bodies;
- dive/fall: establish height, descent direction, gravity and landing state;
- orbit attack: keep the attacker as center, synchronize rotation with one decisive move, avoid face-critical close range;
- dodge: specify attack path, body displacement and stable landing;
- time freeze: state which elements stop, which secondary particles remain suspended and how motion resumes;
- match-shape transition: name the shared shape, the two matching frames and transition duration.

Use at most one dominant camera technique per short beat. Spectacle must clarify power, danger or causality rather than replace them.

## 6. Power and magic effects

Describe supernatural effects through physical behavior:

- origin on or around the body;
- shape and trajectory;
- interaction with clothing, hair, dust, architecture and shadows;
- impact result and final pose;
- color linked to the story's established power system.

Labels such as `angry golden eyes`, `thunder punch` or `earth-splitting strike` are shorthand only. Expand them into visible action and environmental response. Avoid piling multiple unrelated powers into one beat.

## 7. Injury and material continuity

Treat wounds as continuity assets. Record location, side, depth, color, moisture, contamination and healing stage. Describe only what the chosen shot size can reveal.

- abrasion: disrupted surface texture, light blood traces and attached dust;
- fresh cut: visible opening with controlled fresh blood and direction of flow;
- bruise/swelling: localized volume change and plausible discoloration;
- deep wound: use only when the script requires it and keep surrounding tissue behavior physically coherent;
- dried/scabbed wound: darker residue, reduced shine and roughened texture;
- soot/dirt: surface contamination without inventing a wound.

Keep the wound on the same anatomical side across shots. Lighting may change visibility but must not change the wound state. Do not intensify gore beyond the script.

## 8. Detail endings

A close-up or insert should finish the dramatic sentence. Good ending details include a hand tightening on a prop, a cup reaching the lips, a blade stopping, a message appearing in a post-production text zone, a wound revealed, or an eye line settling on a threat.

Do not end on a decorative macro if it adds no evidence, decision or handoff. The last frame must provide a stable visual or sound relay for the next shot.

## 9. Prompt conversion rule

Translate the selected director decision into observable generation language:

`dramatic purpose → composition and scale → subject position/action → one camera or focus change → motivated light → physical feedback → end state`

Keep catalogs, technique names and alternative options out of the final copy-ready prompt. The final prompt contains only the choices actually used for that scene.
